### Threat Model

**Cryptology** consists of two fields:

- **Cryptography:** Converting **plaintext** (the readable message) into **ciphertext** so that messages can be sent securely over an insecure medium such as the Internet.
- **Cryptanalysis:** Recovering plaintext from ciphertext by finding weaknesses or information leaks in a cryptographic system.

The standard model has two legitimate endpoints, **Alice** and **Bob**, communicating over a channel controlled by an intruder, **Trudy**, who may intercept, delete, or add both data and control messages

**Attacker Capabilities**

- **Eavesdropping:** Intercepting messages.
- **Insertion:** Actively inserting messages into a connection.
- **Impersonation:** Spoofing the source address or any other field in a packet.
- **Hijacking:** Taking over an ongoing connection by removing the sender or receiver and taking their place.
- **Denial of Service:** Preventing others from using a service, e.g., by overloading its resources.

**Security Goals (CIA)**

| **Goal** | **Prevents Trudy from** |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Confidentiality** | Reading Alice's messages |
| **Integrity** | Modifying Alice's messages without being detected |
| **Authenticity** | Impersonating Alice |

The standard CIA triad defines the A as **Availability** instead, i.e., keeping a service usable under attack. Cryptography does not by itself guarantee availability, since it cannot stop DoS traffic from exhausting a resource.

### Symmetric Key Encryption

Sender and receiver share the same secret key $K$:

$$C \leftarrow SEnc(K, M) \qquad\qquad M \leftarrow SDec(K, C)$$

- **Advantage:** Efficient.
- **Problem:** Both parties must already share $K$, which requires a separate key exchange.


Eg: **Mono-alphabetic Substitution Cipher**: Each letter is replaced by a fixed other letter, so the key is a mapping from the set of 26 letters to itself. For example:

```
plaintext:  abcdefghijklmnopqrstuvwxyz
ciphertext: mnbvcxzasdfghjklpoiuytrewq

plaintext:  bob i love you alice
ciphertext: nkn s gktc wky mgsbc
```

A general substitution has $26! \approx 4 \times 10^{26}$ keys, but is still insecure: each plaintext letter always maps to the same ciphertext letter, so letter frequencies and repetition patterns survive encryption (`bob` $\rightarrow$ `nkn`) and can be used to recover the mapping without trying every key.

A **shift cipher** (e.g., every letter $+12$) is the special case with only 25 non-trivial keys (with +0 being the trivial one). 

---
### Perfect vs. Computational Security

- **Perfect Security:** The ciphertext reveals nothing about the plaintext, even to an adversary with unlimited computation.
- **Computational Security:** Used by most cryptosystems. The system can certainly be broken given enough work, and the goal is to make that work infeasible.

In the worst case for the defender, the attacker has to try every key, so the required work depends on key length. This bound only holds if no **shortcut** exists that is faster than brute force.

**Brute-Force Time by Key Size**

Worst-case time to try every key, assuming 17 million keys/s per computer, 100 computers for a lab, and the equivalent of 300,000 computers for BOINC (the largest computing grid):

| **Key Size** | **Possible Keys** | **One Computer** | **One Lab** | **BOINC** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **40 bits** (long-time US export limit) | $2^{40} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{12}$ | 18 hours | 11 minutes | 200 ms |
| **56 bits** (DES) | $2^{56} \approx 7.2 \times 10^{16}$ | 134 years | 16 months | 4 hours |
| **128 bits** (modern standard) | $2^{128} \approx 3.4 \times 10^{38}$ | $6.35 \times 10^{23}$ years | $6 \times 10^{21}$ years | $2 \times 10^{18}$ years |
The Universe is about $1.3 \times 10^{10}$ years old.

### Diffie-Hellman (DH) Key Exchange

Key exchange can be done either with DH. Without an existing secure channel, DH lets two parties derive the same key by exchanging information over an insecure channel, such that a third party observing the exchange cannot derive the key.

- **Public values:** A modulus $p$ and a base $g$, agreed on over the insecure channel.
- **Private values:** Alice's secret $a$ and Bob's secret $b$, which are never transmitted.

| **Step** | **Alice** | **Bob** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pick secret** | $a$ | $b$ |
| **Send** | $A = g^a \bmod p$ | $B = g^b \bmod p$ |
| **Compute** | $B^a = (g^b)^a \bmod p$ | $A^b = (g^a)^b \bmod p$ |
| **Shared key** | $g^{ab} \bmod p$ | $g^{ab} \bmod p$ |

**Why the Modulus is Required**

Trudy sees $g$, $p$, $A$, and $B$. Multiplying the public values only gives $g^{a+b}$, not $g^{ab}$, so she needs to recover $a$ or $b$. Without a modulus, this is trivial: if $g = 2$ and $A = 8$, then $a = \log_2 8 = 3$. Under a modulus this becomes the **discrete logarithm problem**, for which no efficient classical algorithm is known when $p$ is suitably large. The value $p = 17$ above is only illustrative and can be brute-forced by hand.

**Man-in-the-Middle Attack**

DH protects against a **passive** adversary, but not an **active** one, because neither party can verify whom the received value came from. Trudy picks her own secrets $a'$ and $b'$ and intercepts the exchange:

1. Alice sends $g^a$. Trudy drops it and sends $g^{a'}$ to Bob.
2. Bob sends $g^b$. Trudy drops it and sends $g^{b'}$ to Alice.
3. Alice computes $g^{ab'}$, which Trudy also computes as $(g^a)^{b'}$.
4. Bob computes $g^{a'b}$, which Trudy also computes as $(g^b)^{a'}$.

Alice and Bob now each share a different key with Trudy, while believing they share one with each other. Trudy decrypts every message with one key, reads or modifies it, and re-encrypts it with the other. The fix is to authenticate the DH messages (see Perfect Forward Secrecy below).

### Public Key/Asymmetric Encryption

**Problem with symmetric encryption:** both parties need the same key $K$ beforehand. To get it over an insecure channel, one needs DH (which is vulnerable to man-in-the-middle unless authenticated) or some earlier secure exchange.

**Idea:** Use two different keys, one for encrypting and one for decrypting. The encryption key is made public, the decryption key is kept secret.

**Algorithms** ($1^k$ is just the security parameter, controlling key length):

| Algorithm | Run by | Output |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $(pk, sk) \leftarrow KeyGen(1^k)$ | Receiver (Bob), once | Public key $pk$ and secret key $sk$ |
| $C \leftarrow AEnc(pk, M)$ | Sender (Alice) | Ciphertext $C$ |
| $M \leftarrow ADec(sk, C)$ | Receiver (Bob) | Plaintext $M$ |
**Protocol**
1. Bob runs $KeyGen$, keeps $sk$ private, and publishes $pk$ (anyone, including Trudy, may see it)
2. Alice computes $C = AEnc(pk_B, M)$ and sends $C$
3. Bob computes $M = ADec(sk_B, C)$

- Knowing $pk$ lets anyone **encrypt**, but not **decrypt**. $pk$ and $sk$ are mathematically related, yet computing $sk$ from $pk$ is computationally infeasible (e.g., RSA relies on the hardness of factoring a large product of two primes, as DH relies on the discrete logarithm problem). 
- No shared secret is needed beforehand, the only requirement is that Alice has the *correct* $pk_B$.
- **Disadvantage:** Much slower than symmetric encryption, and limited to short messages. Both follow from how the scheme computes, shown here for RSA, where $pk = (n, e)$, $sk = d$ and $n$ is a 2048-bit number:
$$C = M^e \bmod n \qquad\qquad M = C^d \bmod n$$
	- **Length limit:** $M$ is treated as one integer, and all arithmetic is done mod $n$. If $M \geq n$, the result is reduced to $M \bmod n$, so decryption would return a different number than $M$. Hence $M < n$, i.e., at most 2048 bits = 256 bytes. The required padding uses some of this, leaving about 245 bytes.
	- **Speed:** Computing $C^d \bmod n$ takes about 2048 squarings and multiplications of 2048-bit integers, each of which is itself many word-level operations. So RSA is orders of magnitude slower per byte.


**The Key Authenticity Problem**

Public keys are not secret, so nothing in the scheme tells Alice whose key she holds. If Trudy replaces $pk_B$ with $pk_T$ in transit, Alice encrypts to $pk_T$, Trudy decrypts with $sk_T$, reads $M$, and can re-encrypt it with the real $pk_B$ and forward it. Alice and Bob notice nothing. The encryption is correct, the key is bound to the wrong identity. This is solved with certificates issued by a CA (see Certification Authority, after Digital Signatures, since certificates are built from signatures).

### Hybrid Encryption

Neither scheme is sufficient alone:

| | Symmetric | Asymmetric |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Speed / message length | Fast, any length | Slow, short messages only |
| Key distribution | Needs a pre-shared key | Needs only the receiver's $pk$ |

Hybrid encryption uses asymmetric encryption only to deliver a fresh symmetric key $K$, and symmetric encryption for the message. Since $K$ is short (e.g., 128 or 256 bits), it fits within the asymmetric length limit, and the long message is handled by the fast cipher.

| Step | Sender (Alice) | Receiver (Bob) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1. Generate session key | $K \leftarrow \{0,1\}^n$ (random) | |
| 2. Protect the key | $C_1 \leftarrow AEnc(pk_B, K)$ | |
| 3. Encrypt the message | $C_2 \leftarrow SEnc(K, M)$ | |
| 4. Transmit | Sends $(C_1, C_2)$ | |
| 5. Recover the key | | $K \leftarrow ADec(sk_B, C_1)$ |
| 6. Decrypt the message | | $M \leftarrow SDec(K, C_2)$ |

- Only Bob can recover $K$ (needs $sk_B$), so only Bob can decrypt $C_2$. Trudy sees $C_1$ and $C_2$ but has neither $K$ nor $sk_B$.
- The asymmetric cost is a single operation on the $n$-bit key $K$, regardless of the message length. Encrypting $M$ directly would need one operation per ~245-byte block, i.e., a cost that grows with $|M|$. In hybrid encryption, only the symmetric part grows with $|M|$, and it is cheap.
- $K$ is generated fresh by the sender each time, so no key has to be agreed upon beforehand, and compromising one $K$ exposes only the messages encrypted under it.

### Data Integrity

Encryption does not by itself provide integrity, so modification in transit must be detected separately. The simplest approach is a checksum, e.g., adding up all the bytes of a message. The last digits of credit card numbers and ISBNs are usually checksums. Alice appends the checksum to the message before encrypting it, and Bob verifies it after decrypting.

Checksums are designed to catch accidental errors. A deliberate attacker can easily construct a different message with the same sum, which is why cryptographic hash functions are used instead.

---
### Cryptographic Hash Functions

A hash function $h$ is a **one-way function** that takes an arbitrary-length string $x$ and computes a fixed-length **message digest** $y = h(x)$. Examples are MD5, RIPEMD, SHA-1, SHA-2, and SHA-3. MD5 and SHA-1 have practical collision attacks and should no longer be used where collision resistance matters.

- **Pre-image Resistance:** Given $h(X)$, it is infeasible to find $X$.
- **Collision Resistance:** It is infeasible to find two inputs $X_1 \neq X_2$ such that $h(X_1) = h(X_2)$

A bare hash Is not enough. Suppose Alice sends $(M, h(M))$ and Bob checks that the hash matches. $h$ is public and has no key, so Trudy can intercept, replace $M$ with $M'$, compute $h(M')$ herself, and send $(M', h(M'))$. Bob's check passes. A bare hash only detects accidental errors, like a checksum. The fix is to make the tag something Trudy cannot compute for $M'$, i.e., it must depend on a secret she does not have. There are two ways: a **MAC**, which uses a key shared between Alice and Bob (below), or a **digital signature**, which uses a key pair (next section).

**Message Authentication Code (MAC)**

Alice and Bob share a secret key $K$.

$$T \leftarrow MAC(K, M)$$

Alice sends $(M, T)$. Bob recomputes $MAC(K, M)$ with his copy of $K$ and accepts only if it equals $T$. Trudy does not know $K$, so she cannot compute a valid $T'$ for $M'$. A MAC is built from a hash function (e.g., HMAC, which hashes $M$ together with $K$ in a specific nested way).

- Needs a pre-shared key.
- Both Alice and Bob can compute the tag, so Bob cannot prove to a third party that Alice (and not he himself) produced it. Bob is convinced because only he and Alice know $K$, but a third party sees a valid $(M, T)$ that Bob could equally have made himself. A MAC therefore does not provide **non-repudiation**, whereas a digital signature does (next section).

---
### Digital Signatures

**Authenticity** means that $M$ comes from the sender, which also implies **integrity**, i.e., $M$ is unmodified. A digital signature provides this with a key pair instead of a shared key: the sender's secret key produces the signature, and the sender's public key checks it.

| Algorithm | Run by | Output |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $(pk, sk) \leftarrow KeyGen(1^k)$ | Sender (Alice), once | Public key $pk$ and secret key $sk$ |
| $S \leftarrow Sign(sk, M)$ | Sender (Alice) | Signature $S$ |
| $\{0,1\} \leftarrow Verify(pk, M, S)$ | Receiver (Bob) | 1 if $S$ is a valid signature on $M$ under $pk$, else 0 |

The receiver can obtain the sender's $pk$ from anywhere, but must verify that it really belongs to the sender using $Cert(pk)$ issued by a CA (see Certification Authority below).

**Hash-then-Sign**

Alice computes $h(M)$ and signs that, so the expensive public key operation is on a fixed-size input: $S = Sign(sk_A, h(M))$. She sends $(M, S)$, with $M$ in the clear (confidentiality is not needed here). Bob recomputes $h(M)$ and runs $Verify$ with $pk_A$ to check that $S$ is a valid signature on it.

- The roles are the reverse of encryption: the secret key signs and the public key verifies.
- $S$ does not hide anything. It is sent alongside $M$, and anyone with $pk_A$ can verify it. What it guarantees is that only the holder of $sk_A$ could have produced a valid $S$ for this $M$.
- No shared key is needed, and since only Alice can produce the signature, it proves the origin to anyone (**non-repudiation**, which a MAC lacks).
- Slower than a MAC.
- For confidentiality as well, Alice encrypts $(M, S)$ for Bob afterwards (sign-then-encrypt, as in PGP).

**RSA Signatures**

With RSA, $pk = (n, e)$ and $sk = d$, where $e$ and $d$ are chosen so that $(x^d)^e \equiv x \pmod n$ for any $x < n$.

| | Computation |
| :--- | :--- |
| $Sign(sk, M)$ | $S = h(M)^d \bmod n$ |
| $Verify(pk, M, S)$ | Compute $h(M)$ and $S^e \bmod n$. Output 1 if they are equal, otherwise 0. |

- **Correctness:** For a valid $S$, $S^e = (h(M)^d)^e \equiv h(M) \pmod n$, so $Verify$ outputs 1.
- **Modified message:** If $M$ is changed to $M'$, Bob computes $h(M') \neq h(M)$ (collision resistance), which does not match $S^e \bmod n = h(M)$, so $Verify$ outputs 0.
- **Why Trudy cannot forge:** A valid $S'$ for a new $M'$ must satisfy $S'^e \equiv h(M') \pmod n$, which requires computing $h(M')^d \bmod n$. This needs $d$, and finding $d$ from $(n, e)$ requires factoring $n$.
- **Padding:** Real RSA signatures pad $h(M)$ to a fixed format first (e.g., RSA-PSS), and $Verify$ also checks the padding. The plain version above has known forgery attacks.
- **Other schemes:** DSA and ECDSA compute differently but have the same $KeyGen$, $Sign$, $Verify$ interface.
- The computation is the same mod-$n$ exponentiation as RSA encryption, which is why it has the same cost, but here the key roles are swapped ($d$ is applied first, and everyone can apply $e$).

**Security Property (Unforgeability):** Given $pk$ and any number of valid pairs $(M_1, S_1), \dots, (M_n, S_n)$, the adversary cannot output a valid signature on a new message $M \notin \{M_1, \dots, M_n\}$.

### Certification Authority (CA)

Public keys (for encryption or for verifying signatures) are only useful if the user knows which identity they belong to. A **CA** is a trusted third party that vouches for this binding. It does so with a digital signature, so a CA provides **authenticity/integrity of public keys**, which supports both encryption and signatures. It does not provide confidentiality by itself.

**Issuing and Checking a Certificate**

1. The CA has its own key pair $(pk_{CA}, sk_{CA})$. $pk_{CA}$ is distributed in advance with the OS or browser, so Alice already has it.
2. Bob proves his identity to the CA and gives it $pk_B$.
3. The CA signs the pair with $sk_{CA}$ and returns the certificate ($\|$ is concatenation):
$$Cert(pk_B) = \big(ID_B,\ pk_B,\ Sign(sk_{CA},\ ID_B \| pk_B)\big)$$
4. Alice obtains $Cert(pk_B)$ from Bob or from anywhere else (the source does not matter) and checks $Verify(pk_{CA}, ID_B \| pk_B, \text{signature})$. This verification is easy to implement and uses the same exponentiation and prime factorisation idea as RSA.

Trudy cannot produce a valid certificate for $(ID_B, pk_T)$ without $sk_{CA}$ (unforgeability), so the substitution is detected. Signing $ID_B \| pk_B$ together, rather than $pk_B$ alone, is what ties the key to the identity.

**Why $pk_{CA}$ Is Trusted**

Alice can only check $Cert(pk_B)$ if she has the correct $pk_{CA}$. To check $pk_{CA}$ she would need a certificate for it, signed by another key, which would itself need checking, and so on. This chain cannot be verified indefinitely, so it must end at keys that are trusted without a certificate. These are called **trust anchors**.

- **Root CAs are the trust anchors.** Their public keys ship with the OS or browser in the **root store**. A root's own certificate is self-signed (signed with its own $sk$), which anyone could do and therefore proves nothing. It is trusted only because the software vendor put it in the store. So trust in roots rests on the integrity of the software download and the vendor's choice of CAs, not on cryptography.

- **Chain of trust.** Root $sk$ keys are kept offline, so day-to-day certificates are issued by **intermediate CAs**, whose certificates are signed by a root. Alice verifies each link in the chain:
	1. $Cert(pk_B)$ is signed by the intermediate CA: check it with $pk_{int}$.
	2. $Cert(pk_{int})$ is signed by the root: check it with $pk_{root}$.
	3. $pk_{root}$ is in Alice's root store: accept.

- **Weakness.** Any CA in the root store can issue a valid certificate for any identity. If a CA is compromised or issues one wrongly, Trudy gets a certificate for $(ID_B, pk_T)$ that verifies. Certificate Transparency logs (public append-only records of issued certificates) and certificate revocation are used to detect and limit this. (**DID NOT UNDERSTAND**)

### Authentication Protocols

Goal: Bob wants Alice to prove her identity to him over a network, where he cannot directly observe who is sending.

| **Protocol** | **Alice's Proof** | **Failure** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **ap1.0** | Sends "I am Alice" | Trudy sends the same message. |
| **ap2.0** | Sends "I am Alice" in an IP packet with her source address | Trudy spoofs Alice's source address. |
| **ap3.0** | Sends "I am Alice" with her secret password | Trudy sniffs the password, or records the packet and plays it back. |
| **ap3.0 (encrypted)** | Sends "I am Alice" with her encrypted password | **Playback attack still works:** Trudy does not need to decrypt the password, only to resend the recorded ciphertext. |

The encrypted password is always the same value, so it proves that someone once knew the password, not that Alice is live now. One fix is to encrypt the password together with a timestamp, $f(K, ts)$, so that Bob rejects stale values. This requires synchronised clocks and still allows a replay within the accepted time window.

**ap4.0: Nonce with a Shared Key**

A **nonce** is a number $R$ used only once-in-a-lifetime.

1. Alice $\rightarrow$ Bob: "I am Alice"
2. Bob $\rightarrow$ Alice: $R$
3. Alice $\rightarrow$ Bob: $K_{A-B}(R)$ (R encrypted under shared key K)

Since $R$ is fresh, the reply must have been computed just now, which proves liveness and defeats playback. Since only Alice (besides Bob) knows $K_{A-B}$, it must be Alice.

**Drawback:** It requires a pre-shared symmetric key. Additionally, it only authenticates the handshake, so messages after it must also be protected with a key, otherwise Trudy can hijack the connection after authentication completes.

**ap5.0: Nonce with Public Key Cryptography**

1. Alice $\rightarrow$ Bob: "I am Alice"
2. Bob $\rightarrow$ Alice: $R$
3. Alice $\rightarrow$ Bob: $K_A^-(R)$, i.e., $R$ signed with her private key
4. Bob $\rightarrow$ Alice: "Send me your public key"
5. Alice $\rightarrow$ Bob: $K_A^+$

Bob computes $K_A^+(K_A^-(R)) = R$. Only the holder of the private key matching $K_A^+$ could have produced this value.

**Failure:** Nothing ties $K_A^+$ to Alice. Trudy can intercept the exchange, answer with $K_T^-(R)$ and $K_T^+$, and Bob's check passes. This is again a man-in-the-middle attack, and it is why public keys must be certified.

**Fix:** In step 5, Alice sends $Cert(K_A^+)$ instead of the bare key. Bob checks it with $pk_{CA}$. Trudy's certificate would name $ID_T$, and she cannot obtain one binding $K_T^+$ to Alice.

Correct primitives do not guarantee a correct protocol, since they must also be composed correctly. Common mistakes include:

- **Using the same stream cipher key for two messages:** XORing the ciphertexts cancels the keystream, giving $C_1 \oplus C_2 = M_1 \oplus M_2$. (**DID NOT UNDERSTAND, what is stream cipher all of a sudden and what is the utility**)

### TLS (Transport Layer Security)

Netscape invented **Secure Sockets Layer (SSL)** in the mid-1990s to protect HTTP connections (HTTP + SSL = HTTPS), though the protocol is general enough to protect any TCP-based connection. A competing protocol, S-HTTP, lost out because Netscape and Microsoft both chose HTTPS. SSL went through a few revisions and was eventually standardised as TLS.

TLS provides security to the transport connection, but is implemented in libraries linked into the application, which is why the Application Layer chapter describes it as an application-layer enhancement of TCP.

![[Screenshot 2026-11-01 TLS connection establishment.png]]

The handshake has two goals: both sides derive the same symmetric keys, and the client confirms it is talking to the real server. The diagram has three phases, A, B and C, with the messages in order below.

**Phase A: Key Exchange (plaintext)**

1. **ClientHello:** The client sends the TLS versions and the list of algorithms it supports (**offered-algorithms-list**), a random **client-nonce**, and its **client-key-share**. The key share is its DH public value (e.g., $g^a \bmod p$). Instead of or along with the key share, it can send a **PSK label**, which names a pre-shared key left over from an earlier session so that a new session can resume with it.

2. **ServerHello:** The server picks one of the offered options and replies with its **server-nonce** and **server-key-share** ($g^b \bmod p$), and/or the PSK label it selected.

After the ServerHello, both sides hold both DH values, so both can compute the same DH secret. They derive the symmetric session keys from it, together with the nonces and a hash of the handshake messages so far. Each input has a different role:

- **`g^{ab}`** is the only secret input. All the secrecy of the keys comes from it.
- **The nonces** are public, so they add no secrecy. They add uniqueness: every connection gets different keys even if the DH values were somehow reused, for example by a server that doesn't generate a fresh `b`. A recorded handshake can't be replayed to reuse an old key.
- **The hash of the handshake messages** is also public. It binds the keys to the exact contents of the hellos. If Trudy alters anything in transit, such as stripping the strong algorithms from the client's offered list, the two sides compute different hashes and therefore different keys. 

Every message after this point is encrypted. 


**Phase B: Server Parameters (encrypted)**

The server sends the remaining connection settings it chose from the client's offers.

**Phase C: Authentication (encrypted)**

DH alone does not say who is on the other end, so it is vulnerable to the man-in-the-middle attack. This phase fixes that:

- **server-certificate:** $Cert(pk_S)$ from a CA. The client checks it against its root store and checks that the name in it matches the site it asked for.
- **server signature:** The server signs the handshake so far with $sk_S$. This proves it holds the secret key for $pk_S$, and since the signed data includes the DH values and nonces, the proof is bound to this specific handshake and cannot be replayed from an earlier one. Trudy cannot sign without $sk_S$, so she cannot insert her own DH value.
- **server-finished-MAC:** A MAC, under the newly derived keys, over the entire handshake. It shows the server derived the same keys, and that no one altered the unencrypted hellos in transit (e.g., Trudy removing the strong algorithms from the client's list to force a weak one).

3. **ClientAgain:** The client sends its own **client-finished-MAC**, with the same purpose. A **client-certificate and signature** are included only if the server requests them, which is uncommon, so by default only the server is authenticated.

**After the Handshake**

- **Application data:** HTTP requests and responses travel over the **TLS channel**, secured by **authenticated encryption**, i.e., encryption and a MAC combined, using the derived symmetric keys. This provides confidentiality and integrity. It is the hybrid pattern: DH and signatures to set up the keys, then fast symmetric cryptography for the bulk data.
- **Close-notify:** Each side sends this authenticated message before terminating the connection, so an attacker cannot silently cut the connection short by injecting a TCP close.

**Primitive Used for Each Goal**

| **Goal** | **Mechanism in TLS** |
| :--- | :--- |
| Confidentiality of data | Symmetric encryption with keys derived from DH |
| Integrity of data | MAC inside authenticated encryption |
| Server identity | Certificate (CA) and signature |
| Handshake not tampered with | Finished MACs |
| Freshness | Nonces |

### PGP (Pretty Good Privacy)

The first popular implementation of public-key cryptography, made by Phil Zimmermann in 1991, at a time when cryptography was highly controlled. Its primary use is to protect the contents of email messages, by providing:
- **Encryption** of messages, using hybrid encryption.
- **Digital signatures** on messages, using hash-then-sign, i.e., signing $h(M)$ instead of $M$.

Each user holds four kinds of keys:

| **Key** | **Visibility** | **Used to** |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Encryption key** | Public | Encrypt messages to the key's owner |
| **Decryption key** | Private | Decrypt messages received by the owner |
| **Signature key** | Private | Sign messages and other people's keys |
| **Verification key** | Public | Verify the owner's signatures |

**Sending a Message**

Alice, sending to Bob:

1. Writes the message.
2. Signs it with **her own signature key**.
3. Encrypts both the message and the signature with **Bob's public encryption key**.

Bob, on receiving it:

1. Decrypts it with **his private decryption key** to obtain the message and signature.
2. Checks the signature with **Alice's verification key**.

### Key Distribution Without CAs

Alice needs an authentic copy of Bob's public key. Without a CA, Bob could publish the key on his webpage, but this alone is not enough, since Trudy could modify the page or the download.

**Fingerprints**

Alice could download the key and verify it with Bob over the phone, but keys are too long to read out. A **fingerprint** is a cryptographic hash of a key, which is much shorter:

```
B117 2656 DFF9 83C3 042B C699 EB5A 896A 2898 8BF5
```

With a collision-resistant hash function, there is no known way to make two different keys with the same fingerprint. Therefore:

1. Alice downloads Bob's key from his webpage.
2. Alice's software calculates the fingerprint.
3. Alice phones Bob, who reads out his key's actual fingerprint.
4. If they match, Alice has an authentic copy of the key.

This does not work for Carol, who might not know Bob well enough to phone him.

**Key Signing and the Web of Trust**

Once Alice has verified Bob's key, she signs it with her signature key. This is effectively a signed message: "I have verified that the key with this fingerprint really belongs to Bob". Bob attaches this signature, along with any others he collects, to the key on his webpage.

Alice now acts as an **introducer** for Bob. If Carol has verified Alice's key and trusts her to introduce people, then Carol can download Bob's key, see Alice's signature, and use the key without checking with Bob personally. This is the **Web of Trust**, which the PGP software handles mostly automatically.

**Issues with Key Signing**

- A signature is only as reliable as the signer's verification, so a careless or compromised introducer vouches for a wrong key.
- Published signatures reveal who knows whom, exposing the users' social graph.

### Problems with PGP

**Problem 1: Usability**

Common mistakes:
- Encrypting the message with the sender's public key instead of the recipient's.
- Sending their private key so that the recipient can decrypt the message.

**Problem 2: Key Compromise**

Suppose Trudy records the encrypted traffic between Alice and Bob, and later obtains all of Bob's key material by stealing his computer or breaking into it (via a virus, trojan, etc.). She can now:
- Decrypt the past messages, since Bob's long-term private key recovers each message key.
- Learn their content and that Alice sent them.
- Show a mathematical proof of this to anyone else, since the signatures remain verifiable.

PGP creates long-lived incriminating records: key material that decrypts data sent over the public Internet, and signatures proving who said what. Alice's privacy therefore depends on Bob's actions, including how well he secures his machine, which she cannot control.

### Off-the-Record (OTR) Communication

A private in-person conversation has the following properties, which signed email does not provide:
- No one else can hear it, unless it is being recorded.
- No one else knows what was said, unless Alice or Bob tells them.
- No one can prove what was said, not even Alice or Bob.

Such a conversation is **off-the-record**. It has legal support in person and over the phone, where recording without notification and tapping phone lines are illegal. Achieving it over the Internet does not require new primitives, only a different use of the existing ones:

- **Perfect forward secrecy**
- **Future secrecy**
- **Deniable authentication**

---
### Perfect Forward Secrecy (PFS)

Future key compromises should not reveal past communication. Suppose Trudy records all encrypted traffic, and later steals Bob's long-term secret key. With PFS she still cannot decrypt the recordings.

**Without PFS (PGP's hybrid encryption):** 

Each message carries $C_1 = AEnc(pk_B, K)$ and $C_2 = SEnc(K, M)$. After stealing $sk_B$, Trudy computes $ADec(sk_B, C_1) = K$ and then decrypts $C_2$. Every past message is exposed, because the long-term key protects each $K$.

**With PFS:**

1. Encrypt messages using symmetric-key encryption with a short-lived **session key**.
2. Create the session key with DH, using fresh random secrets in every session: Alice picks $a$ and sends $g^a$, Bob picks $b$ and sends $g^b$, and both compute $g^{ab}$.
3. Use **long-term keys only to authenticate the DH messages** (by signing $g^a$ and $g^b$), which also prevents the man-in-the-middle attack. They never encrypt or carry the session key.
4. Discard $a$, $b$, and the session key after use, securely erasing them from memory and everywhere else possible.

**Why past traffic stays safe:** Trudy's recording contains $g^a$, $g^b$, and the ciphertext. Computing $g^{ab}$ from $g^a$ and $g^b$ requires solving the discrete logarithm problem, which is infeasible, and $a$ and $b$ no longer exist. A stolen long-term signing key lets Trudy impersonate its owner from then on, but it contains nothing about old session keys.

**The key idea:** The long-term key never protects the session key, it only authenticates. In PGP the long-term key decrypts $K$, so compromising it exposes everything.

---
### Future Secrecy

PFS protects the past. Future secrecy protects the future: if Trudy learns the current session key (e.g., by reading it from memory), messages sent after the key material has been refreshed should be safe again, so the protocol recovers from a compromise.

**Without it:** If all session keys were derived from one initial DH exchange, learning that one key (or the secrets behind it) would expose every later message too.

**With future secrecy:**

1. Alice and Bob keep exchanging fresh DH values alongside their messages. Each new value is $g^{a'}$ or $g^{b'}$, where $a'$ and $b'$ are new random numbers.
2. Each new session key is derived from the latest shared secret, e.g., $g^{a'b'}$.
3. Old keys and secrets are deleted as in PFS.

**Why it recovers:** $a'$ and $b'$ are fresh random numbers, not computed from the old keys, so knowing the old key tells Trudy nothing about them. She only sees $g^{a'}$ and $g^{b'}$ on the wire, and computing $g^{a'b'}$ from those is the discrete logarithm problem. Once one fresh exchange completes after the compromise, later messages are protected again. This holds as long as Trudy only observes. If she steals a long-term signing key, she can impersonate its owner and take part in the new exchange herself.

**The key idea:** PFS and future secrecy together mean a key compromise exposes only the messages in the window around it, not everything before or after.

---
### Deniable Authentication

During the conversation, Bob is convinced that Alice is the sender, but he cannot prove this to anyone else. This is the electronic form of "no one can prove what was said, not even Alice or Bob".

**Why signatures fail:** If Alice signs her messages, $Verify(pk_A, M, S) = 1$ convinces anyone, since only Alice could have produced $S$. Bob can show $(M, S)$ to a third party as proof. This non-repudiation is useful for contracts, and is the opposite of what an off-the-record conversation needs.

**Why a MAC works:**

1. Alice and Bob share the key $K$ (from the DH exchange) and Alice sends $(M, MAC(K, M))$.
2. Bob checks the MAC. He is convinced, since he knows he did not make it and only Alice has the other copy of $K$.
3. A third party sees a valid pair $(M, T)$ but knows Bob also holds $K$. Bob could have produced $T$ for any $M$ himself, so the transcript is not evidence against Alice.

**Why this does not undo the long-term keys:** The long-term signature in the DH step covers only the DH values $g^a$ and $g^b$, not the messages. It shows that Alice took part in some exchange, but ties her to nothing she said.

OTR goes further: once a MAC key has been used and the messages read, it publishes that key in a later message. After that, anyone could have forged earlier messages, so a transcript is worthless even to a third party who suspects Bob did not forge it. (The slides do not cover this detail.)

**The key idea:** Authentication that depends on a secret both parties know convinces each party but nobody else. Authentication that depends on a secret only the sender holds (a signature) convinces everyone.
